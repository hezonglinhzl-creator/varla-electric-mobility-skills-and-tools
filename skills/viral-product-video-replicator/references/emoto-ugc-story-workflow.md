# Electric-Motorcycle UGC Story Workflow

Use this reference for electric motorcycle, electric dirt bike, or e-bike narrative videos. It covers family surprise, garage reveal, one-take contrast, crowd reaction, skill-showcase framing, and product-led relationship comedy.

## 1. Start From Evidence

Before writing, identify:

- product model and supplied reference images
- non-negotiable product details: color, frame, seat, tires, brakes, chain/belt, foot pegs, controller/display, headlight, logo, ignition method
- requested runtime and whether the output is one take or split clips
- characters already established in prior assets
- platform and the requested deliverable: plot, generation prompt, character sheet, or caption

If a model-specific detail is not visible or documented, mark it as unknown. Never copy details from another model merely because an earlier prompt used them.

## 2. Convert Plot Into Feasible Beats

Build around one emotional path:

`hook -> suspicion or desire -> product interaction -> reveal/proof -> reaction -> payoff`

For a 14-16 second one-take video, prefer 4-6 readable beats and no more than three short dialogue moments. Spoken English usually needs breathing room; reduce dialogue before reducing the visual proof. If the plot requires a location change, purchase sequence, relationship development, or time jump, split it into clips or use a concise montage rather than falsely calling it one continuous take.

Every beat must specify:

- time range
- camera position and continuous movement
- who is visible
- product state and action
- one dominant sound
- start state and end state

The camera path must be physically continuous. Do not combine an exterior wide shot, ignition close-up, riding follow shot, and reverse reaction angle in one take unless the operator can plausibly move between them.

### Primary Prompt Style: Clear Two-Second Narrative Blocks

Use the proven `one drop of sweat accidentally bought a bike` prompt structure as the primary writing model for future electric-motorcycle stories. Its strength is clear cause and effect, fixed visual continuity, short timed paragraphs, and dialogue embedded directly inside visible action.

Default structure:

1. **Format and capture lock:** duration, 9:16, realistic UGC, Apple iPhone rear 1x, frame rate, natural exposure, handheld behavior, location, light, and no cinematic or commercial look.
2. **Character lock:** one compact paragraph per main character covering identity, body, face, hair, clothes, current physical state, and behavioral personality.
3. **Timed narrative:** write one continuous prose paragraph for every approximately two seconds. A 10-second clip normally uses five blocks; an 8-second clip normally uses four blocks.
4. **Continuity sentence:** for later clips, explicitly preserve the previous clip's people, clothes, product, environment, light, emotional residue, and prop positions.
5. **Final logic sentence:** state where the comedy or emotion comes from and reiterate the realistic phone-video style.

Every timed paragraph must clearly describe:

- camera framing or cut
- physical action and prop interaction
- starting facial state
- emotional trigger and visible facial change
- one or two coherent micro-actions or body reactions
- exact dialogue and speaker
- the end state that leads into the next time block

Write these elements as natural narrative prose, not as a long technical checklist. Use concrete observable language. For example, replace `he is shocked` with `his thumb stops above the screen; he freezes for half a second, blinks twice, raises both eyebrows, lets his jaw drop, then looks from the phone to the delivery van before speaking`.

Do not overload a two-second block. Prefer one main action, one emotional transition, and one short line from each active speaker. If a block needs multiple lines, keep them very short and make the turn-taking explicit. Preserve enough silence for facial reaction and physical movement.

The prompt should read like a clear director describing exactly what happens moment by moment. Keep the benchmark's directness and causal rhythm, while adding stronger facial management and body reactions where the original benchmark is under-specified.

### Preferred Multi-Cut Action Structure

Also use the proven three-shot `dog accidentally confirms the order` structure as a reference for shot density, natural action progression, and camera-language variety. Its useful pattern is:

`environmental setup -> tighter action/reaction detail -> wider reveal and shared payoff`

Recommended shot density:

- 6-second clip: normally 3 distinct shots, about 2 seconds each.
- 8-second clip: normally 4 distinct shots.
- 10-second clip: normally 4-5 distinct shots.
- Use fewer shots only when the user explicitly requests one continuous take or when a single emotional performance needs more breathing room.

Each cut must reveal new information or change the viewer's understanding. Do not cut only for visual variety. Use shot-scale contrast:

1. **Setup shot:** environmental medium or wide shot establishes location, people, product position, movement direction, and the starting relationship.
2. **Detail/reaction shot:** cut closer to the hand, phone, face, prop, or product interaction that causes the reversal. Show the trigger and immediate facial change in the same shot when possible.
3. **Reveal/payoff shot:** cut wider or to a reverse angle so the audience sees the consequence, the other character's reaction, and the product clearly together.

Build a physical action chain across cuts. A strong chain has a visible cause, an immediate reaction, and a consequence, for example:

`rider passes -> character looks twice -> character checks phone -> accidental input -> notification sound -> frozen reaction -> delivery vehicle appears -> product rolls into view -> partner turns back -> shared comic reaction`

Natural cut bridges are mandatory:

- action match: a hand moving toward the phone continues in the next close shot
- gaze match: a character looks off-screen and the next shot reveals what they see
- sound bridge: notification, vehicle door, wheel roll, laugh, or dialogue begins just before or continues across the cut
- position continuity: screen direction, character left/right relationship, product orientation, light direction, and prop state remain consistent
- emotional continuity: the next shot begins from the exact facial and body state reached at the end of the previous shot

Camera-movement continuity is also mandatory. Every moving shot must define its start position, movement direction, subject being followed, and end position. The next shot must begin from a spatially compatible position and either continue the same motion or clearly settle before changing direction.

Use motivated handheld movement:

- pan or turn because a character looks toward a new subject
- step closer because a phone, hand, face, or product detail becomes important
- follow sideways because a rider or person moves through the scene
- pull back because a delivery, gift, vehicle, or group reaction must be revealed
- settle briefly before a hard cut so the viewer can read the expression or product

For movement handoffs between shots:

- preserve screen direction: a subject moving left-to-right continues left-to-right after the cut unless a clear neutral reset shot is inserted
- match movement energy: a slow handheld follow should not cut directly to a fast whip or perfectly static tripod frame without story motivation
- match end and start vectors: if the first shot pans right and ends on the phone, the next close shot begins with the phone entering from the corresponding side and continues the hand action
- use foreground objects, shoulders, vehicles, or a character crossing frame to motivate a concealed or natural cut when useful
- keep the 180-degree axis and character left/right relationship stable during dialogue and reaction exchanges
- avoid camera teleportation, unexplained height changes, repeated orbiting, sudden reverse pans, digital zoom pulses, floating gimbal motion, and cinematic crane or drone movement

Before delivery, perform a camera-path pass across adjacent shots: `shot A end position/direction/speed -> cut bridge -> shot B start position/direction/speed`. Rewrite any transition that cannot plausibly be captured by the same nearby friend holding an iPhone.

Vary camera language without becoming cinematic: environmental medium shot, handheld follow, over-shoulder phone detail, chest-up reaction close-up, reverse reaction, and wider reveal are useful. Keep all shots plausible for the same nearby friend holding an iPhone. Avoid impossible camera teleportation, drone views, stabilized commercial tracking, repeated identical mid-shots, or abrupt axis reversal.

Within every two-second block, describe the action in the order it happens. Do not write simultaneous actions that a person cannot physically complete in time. The order should be readable, such as `dog licks hand -> nose touches screen -> notification sounds -> man freezes -> he says the line`, not a loose list of unrelated gestures.

For the final payoff, keep both sides of the reaction visible whenever possible: the main character's embarrassment, panic, sadness, or surprise and the partner/family/crowd response. End on a relationship reaction or emotional contrast rather than a product-only advertising pose.

## 3. Native UGC Direction

Use believable phone-video behavior:

- 9:16 vertical, ordinary rear-camera 1x look unless the user specifies otherwise
- handheld micro-shake, imperfect framing, natural auto exposure, mild motion blur
- ordinary garage, driveway, park, or closed activity area with real clutter and background sound
- characters focused on each other or the product, not performing to camera unless the concept is direct-to-camera
- diegetic sound first: footsteps, paper, key jingle, ignition tone, motor hum, tire roll, room tone, laughter, overlapping reactions

Avoid combining conflicting camera language such as phone-wide realism with strong shallow depth of field, stabilized commercial tracking, or cinematic hero lighting.

## 4. Product Continuity

Create a compact product lock and repeat it in every segment:

```text
Product lock: [model], [color], [frame/seat/tire identity], [drive/brake details], [controller/display location], [headlight], [verified ignition method]. Keep normal proportions and the same parts throughout. No redesign, no missing parts, no extra parts, no model substitution.
```

Show product operation only as verified. If the source material confirms a key ignition, show the key entering the correct visible ignition point, turning, the display illuminating, and the headlight responding. If not confirmed, avoid inventing the mechanism.

## 4A. Emotional Contrast And Reaction Direction

For every electric-motorcycle narrative, comedy, relationship story, family story, surprise, reversal, embarrassment, panic, or crowd-response prompt, emotional performance is mandatory by default. Do not wait for the user to request stronger emotion. Do not rely on abstract words such as `surprised`, `excited`, `shocked`, `funny`, or `emotional`. Translate every emotional beat into visible performance.

Every speaking shot and every reaction shot must include all three layers:

1. **Facial management:** specify the eyes, eyebrows, blink pattern, jaw, lips, cheeks, nostrils, and gaze change. The expression must visibly change during the shot instead of staying fixed while the mouth reads dialogue.
2. **Micro-action:** specify at least one small involuntary or situational action, such as swallowing, pressing lips together, wiping sweat twice, gripping and releasing a strap, nearly dropping a phone, rubbing the neck, shifting weight, taking half a step back, glancing away, looking back twice, covering the mouth, or exchanging a look.
3. **Contrast action:** specify how the end of the shot differs from its beginning, such as pretending to be calm and then freezing, holding back laughter and then breaking, acting confident and then collapsing, or looking uninterested and then turning back twice.

These three layers are a hard prompt requirement, not optional decoration. A shot that only contains a line of dialogue plus `he looks surprised` is incomplete and must be rewritten.

Use one clear emotional ladder per main character. A useful comedy ladder is:

`neutral baseline -> trigger recognition -> frozen disbelief -> physical reaction -> attempted recovery -> larger payoff reaction`

For every reaction beat, specify all of the following:

- trigger: the exact sound, object, line, or action that causes the reaction
- latency: a visible 0.2-0.5 second processing pause before dialogue
- face: eyebrows, eyes, jaw, lips, cheeks, blinking, and gaze direction
- body: shoulders, torso, hands, step, recoil, lean, or loss of composure
- voice: pitch shift, breath, pace, voice crack, laugh interruption, or unfinished word
- contrast: how this state is visibly different from the immediately previous state
- hold: keep the peak reaction readable for 0.4-0.8 second before cutting away

Default contrast rules for short comedy:

- Start at 10-20% intensity so later reactions have room to grow.
- First surprise reaches about 60-70% intensity.
- Final reversal reaches 90-100% intensity and must be the largest reaction in the clip.
- Give different characters different reaction shapes. One may freeze and stare while another doubles over laughing; do not make everyone smile, gasp, or move in sync.
- Pair facial change with body change. An open mouth alone is not enough; use a backward step, rigid shoulders, palms opening, phone nearly slipping, a quick head turn, or laughter bending the torso.
- Make expression management broad and highly readable on a phone screen: pronounced eyebrow travel, clear gaze shifts, visible jaw changes, held breath, sudden blinking, cheek movement, lip compression, and delayed release. Preserve real anatomy and natural timing, but do not underplay the reaction.
- Give each character one or two continuous micro-actions per shot. The actions must support the emotion and remain physically coherent across cuts; do not stack unrelated gestures.
- Preserve realistic muscle motion, breathing, asymmetry, and recovery. The performance may be broad, but it must still look like an ordinary person caught on a phone, not a cartoon face or stage actor.
- Hold the reaction before the next line. Dialogue that begins immediately on the trigger often produces a flat face.

Avoid instructions that suppress the requested performance, such as `subtle reaction`, `restrained`, `barely reacts`, `deadpan throughout`, or `natural smile only`, unless that character's low-energy response is intentionally contrasted with another character's high-energy response.

Before delivering any final video prompt, run an emotional-performance pass. For every timed shot, confirm that it contains: `starting expression -> trigger -> processing pause -> facial change -> micro-action -> body reaction -> ending expression`. If any element is missing, add it while keeping dialogue feasible for the runtime.

### Family Dialogue And Gift-Reveal Performance

Family dialogue must use the same expressive standard as comedy. Do not turn warm, sad, disappointed, worried, grateful, or surprised family scenes into quiet talking heads with fixed faces. Keep the acting believable, but make every emotional transition large enough to read clearly on a vertical phone screen.

Use visible performance patterns such as:

- **Sadness or disappointment:** gaze drops, blinking slows, lips press together or tremble slightly, shoulders sink, chest exhales, hands clasp or rub together, fingers tighten around a bag or sleeve, and the character pauses before answering. The character may turn partly away or wipe the corner of an eye, but avoid instant theatrical sobbing unless the story requires it.
- **Unhappiness or frustration:** brows draw inward, jaw tightens, nostrils flare slightly, arms fold and then release, weight shifts away from the other person, the character looks aside before responding, and the voice becomes shorter or lower. Do not express unhappiness with a neutral face and ordinary delivery.
- **Worry or financial pressure:** the character glances from the price or product to the family member, swallows, forces a small smile that quickly disappears, grips a strap or wallet, exhales, and hesitates before speaking.
- **Receiving an unexpected gift:** first freeze for 0.3-0.6 second; eyes widen, eyebrows lift, jaw drops, breath catches, and the gift is checked twice. Then the character looks rapidly between the gift and giver, covers the mouth or places both hands on the chest, steps back or forward, reaches out and pulls back once in disbelief, and finally moves into a broad hug, laugh, tears, or excited hand movement. The surprise must unfold in stages rather than switching instantly to a smile.
- **Gratitude and emotional release:** shoulders drop after tension, eyes become wet, the smile grows unevenly, the character exhales or laughs through the emotion, opens both arms, leans forward, and initiates a full-body hug. The other person must visibly receive the hug rather than remaining still.
- **Family reaction contrast:** one person may freeze, another cover their mouth, another laugh or tear up, and background relatives may exchange looks before joining. Avoid identical synchronized smiling, crying, clapping, or gasping.

For a gift-reveal scene, require three distinct states: `before reveal: unhappy or resigned`, `recognition: frozen disbelief`, `after confirmation: large joyful release`. The final state must differ strongly in face, posture, hand position, voice, and distance between characters. Include at least one substantial body action, such as stepping back in shock, rushing forward, opening both arms, a full embrace, briefly crouching from emotion, or bouncing once with excitement. Keep feet grounded and movement physically plausible; do not use cartoon jumps, face distortion, or uncontrolled collision.

In short family scenes, protect the emotional beats before adding more dialogue. Prefer three or four short lines with readable reaction pauses over dense conversation that leaves no time for expression or body movement.

For crowds, define a reaction cascade rather than a synchronized group response: one person notices first, two turn their heads a fraction later, another covers their mouth, and one exchanges a look with a companion. Keep crowd dialogue indistinct unless it is essential.

## 5. Character Assets And Continuity

For a reusable three-view asset prompt, require:

- one character identity repeated exactly three times
- front, strict side, and back views
- full body and feet visible, equal scale and neutral posture
- identical face, hair, body shape, outfit, shoes, and accessories
- plain white or light-gray background
- realistic skin, age, fabric, asymmetry, and proportions
- no text, labels, product, helmet, or props unless requested

Keep asset state separate from scene state. A no-helmet character sheet may feed a riding scene where the same character later wears an appropriate helmet.

When writing diverse characters, keep ethnicity and body type neutral descriptors. Attach comedy to the situation, choices, awkward timing, or dialogue. Do not make race, body size, sweat, age, or disability cues the explanation for being undesirable, dirty, incompetent, or dangerous.

## 6. Minors, Conflict, And Riding Risk

When a child appears:

- do not depict real hitting, threatened physical punishment, or violent contact
- replace intimidation with a stern look, pointed gesture, misunderstanding, or harmless fake-out
- do not place the child on a vehicle during a stunt or as a passenger in a stunt
- keep the child at a visible safe distance during adult riding and bring them close only after the vehicle has fully stopped
- use age-appropriate protective gear for any ordinary riding

For adult skill-showcase concepts:

- do not write step-by-step stunt technique
- frame production as a qualified adult rider on a controlled closed course with protective equipment
- prefer a brief controlled visual, reaction editing, camera angle, or VFX/compositing over escalating repeated dangerous maneuvers
- never claim that wording guarantees TikTok or Instagram approval

If the user asks to intensify danger, increase crowd energy, reaction sound, camera shake, or edit rhythm instead of increasing the actual hazard.

## 7. Common Story Patterns

### Garage Surprise

Use one clear misdirection, a key handoff, product reveal, verified startup action, and emotional payoff. Do not reward dangerous or abusive behavior. Keep the reveal readable and the vehicle unobstructed.

### Elder Contrast / Crowd Reaction

Build contrast through expectations and composure, not humiliation. Avoid faking disability as a joke. A slower entrance can show age and deliberateness, followed by confident control. Keep minors as observers and intensify the crowd reaction instead of the stunt.

### Product-Led Meet-Cute

A believable short arc is: notices shared interest, obtains or tries the product through a plausible transition, awkward first interaction, shared ride/activity, mutual interest. A purchase, location change, and relationship outcome require montage or multiple clips; do not compress them into an impossible one-take claim.

## 8. Platform Copy

Generate separate copy after the story is locked.

TikTok:

- first line states the expectation or POV
- second line reveals the reversal without explaining every beat
- short, conversational, reaction-led
- use only relevant hashtags

Instagram:

- slightly warmer context and relationship payoff
- retain the same verified product/model naming
- avoid performance claims not shown in the video

Do not use stunt terms or hashtags if the final video does not visibly contain that action. Do not describe an AI-generated character as a real customer.

## 9. Delivery Checklist

Before delivery, verify:

- runtime can contain the actions and dialogue
- one-take camera path is physically coherent, or the output is correctly labeled as split clips/montage
- product model details come from the correct reference
- character identity, clothing, and scene state remain stable
- minors are not placed in dangerous action
- intensity comes from reactions and storytelling, not instructions for hazardous execution
- TikTok and Instagram copy are distinct and factually consistent
